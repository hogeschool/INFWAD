# Case: Hollowmere

Hollowmere is an adventurers' guild of about ninety members, most of them young and not yet very good at it. Their hall stands at the edge of the town. The quest board and the letter box are on the ground floor. The yard is behind the hall, the armoury is in the basement, and the archive is a chest under the stairs. Letters from the villages up the valley arrive in the letter box. Each asks for help with something: the wyvern from the marsh has learned which day the market is, and a ghost in the mill at the ford stops the wheel at midnight. The wolves of the forest road are back too, in a letter from the same farmer as last month. Whichever member gets to the letters first picks one, pins it on the board as a quest and gathers a party. The party goes, and most of the time it returns and hands its gear back to the armoury. It writes down what happened on whatever scrap of paper can be found, sometimes in runes.

The board, the armoury and the chest have kept the guild running for sixty years, but barely. Members fight over who goes on a quest, sometimes twelve of them for six places, and the leader usually just ends it by taking whoever is nearest the door. (Last autumn a member who had been at his dinner while the others planned did not wait to be taken at all, and ran into the cave before the leader had finished. He said afterwards that at least he had eaten.) Rope, lanterns and shields leave the armoury on somebody's back and return months later, or never, and nobody can say which of the swords have come back. Which members have finished their drills in the yard is something only the quartermaster knows, and only from memory. And the reports in the chest are a pile so deep and so badly written that nobody reads them. Last spring a party went to the marsh for the wyvern, not knowing that two parties had already tried in the winter and each had returned one member fewer.

The guild wants one application for the armoury, the quests, the yard and the reports. It does not quite trust the new technology, so anything to do with gold stays out of it and in the treasurer's ledger. The villagers do not get an account. The guild stopped recording classes, levels and ranks on its members years ago, after the argument, and has no wish to start again. And the guild has no smith and no wizard: gear is not conjured, not copied and not mended. Anything broken is thrown into the swamp.

## Members, the quartermaster and the bestiary

Everyone in the guild has a member account, and everything below is done as a member. One member is the quartermaster, who holds the armoury keys. The current one took an arrow to the knee eleven years ago and has kept the armoury ever since, but it could be any member. A member counts as trained once the quartermaster marks them so, after the drills, which are the practice sessions in the yard. That mark decides whether the armoury lends a member dangerous gear, and whether a leader may take them on a dangerous quest.

The bestiary holds the guild's knowledge of creatures. A creature has a name, a region and a description, and it can have a drawing. Two creatures are the same creature only when the name and the region are both the same. The wolves of the forest road and the wolves of the northern range are two entries. The bestiary marks which creatures are dangerous, meaning not for an untrained party, and the quartermaster keeps that mark current. It includes a rabbit in the western meadows, which nobody believes until they have been to the western meadows. Any member reads the bestiary and adds a creature that is new to it. Quests are often against a creature in it, and reports name creatures from it.

## What the application has to do

### The armoury

The armoury holds the guild's gear: rope, lanterns, swords, shields, crossbows, lockpicks and an 800 pound bear trap nobody has dared to set. Each piece is one thing, on the wall or in one member's hands. A member takes a piece on loan for a quest, or for a week of practice, and returns it. The guild has a maximum loan period, and a loan longer than that is late. Some kinds of gear are dangerous in untrained hands, a crossbow as much as the two-handed sword the guild calls Regret, and the armoury marks which.

Every piece has a condition. The note a member writes on returning a piece is for the next member: a sword with a bent tip, a lantern that leaks oil. When a piece is no longer safe the quartermaster retires it, and a retired piece is never lent again, because it is in the swamp.

- A piece of gear that is out on loan cannot be lent again before its return has been recorded, together with a note on its condition. The member who borrowed it or the quartermaster records the return.
- Dangerous gear is lent only to a trained member.
- A member with a late loan, or with three late returns in the past year, cannot start a new loan.
- Only the quartermaster retires a piece of gear, and a retired piece is not lent.

What has to be kept track of for a piece of gear: its name, its kind, its condition, whether it is dangerous and whether it has been retired. For a loan: the piece, the member, the day it started, the day it was returned, and the note on its condition.

### The quest board

A quest is an expedition: a party leaves on a fixed day, with a leader, and comes back. A member accepts a letter from a village and posts the quest, and that member is its leader. The quest names the village and describes the area in a few lines, like the burial mounds in the west valley, haunted according to the letter. It names the creature from the bestiary when there is one, a day of departure, and how many days the party is expected to be gone. The leader also decides the size of the party (the number of members that can join the quest). Members can request a place, and the leader then chooses the members they want. A request not confirmed by the day of departure stays a request, and that member stays home.

Some quests are for anybody, but one does not simply walk into the northern range, where every creature is marked dangerous.

- Only the quest's leader confirms a request for joining the quest.
- The size of the party determines the maximum amount of confirmed members.
- On a quest against a dangerous creature, the leader is a trained member and the leader confirms only trained members.
- Only the leader changes or cancels a quest. The leader can hand the quest to another member, but only to one with a confirmed place.

What has to be kept track of for a quest: the leader, the village, the description of the area, the creature when there is one, the day of departure, the number of days, the number of places (the maximum amount of members that can join), and whether it has been cancelled. For a request: the quest, the member, and whether the leader has confirmed it.

### The yard

The yard behind the hall is where members learn the sword and the crossbow before either is trusted to them. A drill is one session in the yard with a subject, a date and an instructor, the experienced member who runs it. A subject is a name, the sword or the crossbow, and not a piece from the armoury wall. Members register for a drill, and after it the instructor confirms who was there. The drills are what the quartermaster reads before marking anybody trained. The guild would like that history out of the quartermaster's head, because he is getting old, and somewhere a second person can see it.

A drill is run by somebody the guild already trusts with the subject. One member praises the sun from the yard every morning, drill or no drill, and has run the sword drill for as long as anyone remembers. The yard belongs to the armoury, and the quartermaster closes it now and then, for a departure or for repairs. A drill on a closed day is cancelled.

- Attendance at a drill is confirmed only by its instructor.
- A drill is run only by a trained member.
- Only the instructor, or the quartermaster, cancels a drill.

What has to be kept track of for a drill: its subject, its date, its instructor and whether it has been cancelled. For attendance: who registered for which drill and whether the instructor confirmed they were there.

### The archive

A report is what a party writes down after a quest: the region, which is a name from the map and not simply 'yonder', the day of departure, what happened, and its encounters. An encounter is one line per creature met, and what came of it. The reports are the guild's memory, and the reason the bestiary says what it says: the rabbit is in it because of eight reports, each of which gives the reader nightmares for a week.

A member writes a report as a draft and files it when it is done. A member who came home missing an arm once filed it as only a flesh wound, and that remained the way the story is told: once filed, a report stays as it was written, mistakes included. The guild also wants reports filed while the quest is still fresh in memory. One party is still famous for filing its report thirty years after coming home, and the guild has had enough of that.

- A draft report is visible only to the member writing it, and a filed report is visible to every member.
- Filing is final: a filed report does not change afterwards.
- A member starts a new report only when their previous report has been filed.

What has to be kept track of for a report: the writer, the region, the day of departure, the text, and whether it is a draft or filed. For each encounter on a report: the creature and what came of it.
