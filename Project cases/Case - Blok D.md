# Case: Blok D

Blok D is the makerspace on the ground floor of building D, run by the students who use it. On a busy afternoon a dozen members are at work in one big room under a concrete ceiling, and the air smells of burnt wood and warm plastic. Somebody feeds a sheet of plywood into Bertha, the bigger of the two laser cutters. All six 3D printers are running jobs that started before lunch, and the embroidery machine is stitching a rainbow logo onto a bag. Along one wall hang the hand tools, and at the back sits the stock of sheet material and filament. About a hundred students hold a membership. They come in between lectures to cut, print, stitch and solder, for study projects and for their own ideas. A desk near the door is staffed by members who volunteered for that week, and the desk is where everything is arranged.

The space runs on a paper diary at that desk and a group chat, and both have reached their limit. Machine time is claimed by writing a name in the diary, and two members regularly arrive for the same hour on the same machine. Hand tools leave the room and return weeks later, or not at all. Nobody knows how much acrylic is left until the last sheet is gone. And the one condition of membership, documenting what was made and publishing it, lives in the group chat as a promise and rarely as a fact.

The members who run the desk want one application for the whole space: the machine time, the tools, the material and the published projects. The diary and the group chat can then retire. Money never appears in it. The membership fee pays for the material, so nobody pays at the desk for a sheet or a spool.

## Members and machines

Everyone who uses the space has a member account, and everything below is done as a member. The members who staff the desk that week are members like any other, with the desk's work on top.

The machines are the centre of the space. Each machine has a nickname given by the members, a kind, and a status: in service or out of service. A machine goes out of service when it needs repair and comes back when it is repaired, and the desk keeps that status current. Who repairs it is not the members' concern. The list of machines, with what each one is and whether it can be used, is visible to every member.

## What the application has to do

### Machine time

Nobody simply walks up to a machine and starts using it. A member first attends an instruction session for the machine, a short evening workshop led by an experienced member, and books time on it after that. Any member who knows the machine can announce and lead a session for it. Members register for it, and after the evening the leader confirms who attended. One session is enough: a member who has attended one for Bertha can book Bertha from then on. The sessions exist because the machines are expensive and unforgiving. The embroidery machine has ruined two hoodies this semester, both belonging to members who skipped the evening.

A booking is a start and an end time on one machine. A member plans two hours for a long print or twenty minutes for a cutting job, and every member can see what is taken and when. That visibility is half the point, because the arguments at the diary were never about the rules, only about who wrote their name first.

- A machine can be booked only by a member whose attendance at a session for that machine has been confirmed.
- Two bookings on the same machine cannot overlap.
- Attendance at a session is confirmed only by the member who led that session.
- A machine that is out of service cannot be booked.

What has to be kept track of for a session: the machine it is for, the leader and the date. For attendance: who registered for which session and whether the leader confirmed they were there. For a booking: the machine, the member, and the start and end time.

### Tool loans

The tool wall holds what a project needs outside machine hours: drills, sanders, soldering irons, glue guns, calipers, clamps. Unlike the machines, tools may leave the building. A member takes a tool home for a few days to finish something, and the space has a maximum loan period for that.

A loan starts when the tool leaves the wall and ends when it is back. A borrower in a hurry leaves the tool at the desk, and the desk hangs it up for them. Whether a loan is late follows from the maximum period, counted from the day the loan started. Late loans are the reason half the wall is empty on any given Tuesday, and the desk wants them visible.

- A tool that is out on loan cannot be taken by another member before it has been returned.
- A member with a late loan cannot start a new loan.
- The return of a tool is recorded only by the member who borrowed it or by the desk, together with a note on its condition.

The note on the condition is for the next borrower: a soldering iron with a worn tip is worth knowing about in advance.

What has to be kept track of for a tool: its name, its kind and its condition. For a loan: the tool, the member, the day it started, the day the tool came back, and the note on its condition.

### Materials

At the back of the room sit the sheet materials and the filament: plywood, acrylic and cardboard by the sheet, and filament by weight, every kind and colour of it a material of its own. Members take what their project needs and record what they took. Taking material is called a withdrawal at the desk: which material, how much, and who took it. When the space restocks, the delivery comes in at the desk and is recorded there the same way: which material, how much, and on which date.

The space buys in bulk, a material runs out at the worst moment, and nobody saw it coming because nobody counted. Filament is the worst of it: a spool that looked half full on Monday is gone by Thursday. The desk also wants material use to stay fair. The space has a fair-use quantity per material per month, so that one member does not turn a month of filament into a single weekend of printing.

- The stock of a material follows from its deliveries minus its withdrawals, and only the desk records a delivery. The space keeps no running count of it.
- A withdrawal larger than the calculated stock is refused.
- A member who has reached the monthly fair-use quantity for a material cannot withdraw that material again until the next month starts.

What has to be kept track of for a material: its name, its kind and its unit (sheets or grams). For a delivery: the material, the quantity and the date. For a withdrawal: the material, the member, the quantity and the date.

### Projects

The publishing condition is the reason the university gives the space its room. Whoever makes something here documents it and publishes the design, so that the next member can learn from their experience. A project is that documentation. It has a title, a description of what was made and how, and a web address where the design files live. The space hosts no files of its own. A description is enough when it lets somebody remake the thing: the settings that worked, the material that did not, the measurement that turned out to matter. A project also records which machines were used and roughly how long on each, which is how the desk learns what the machines are used for.

While the member is building, the project is theirs alone, a private notebook. Once published, it is available for every member to read, learn from and remake. It also stays as it was. A member once replaced a published design with an improved one, and three others were halfway through remaking the old one at the time. The published projects are the first thing a new member is shown, and for many this sharing of maker-wisdom is the reason they joined.

- A project that is in progress is visible only to the member building it, and a published project is visible to every member.
- Publication is final: a published project does not change afterwards.
- A member can start a new project only when their previous project has been published.

What has to be kept track of for a project: the member building it, its title, its description, the web address of the design, and its status: in progress or published. For each machine used on a project: the time spent on it.
